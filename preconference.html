About

Is Environmental Psychology Research Fit for Purpose?

Environmental psychology has much to contribute to challenges such as climate change and biodiversity loss — but how can we make sure our research actually reaches and supports the people, communities, and organisations working on these issues?

The morning will bring together researchers and practitioners for keynote presentations, a panel discussion, and facilitated conversations about bridging research and practice, building meaningful academic–community collaborations, and developing more place-based and impactful environmental psychology.

We will also have a dedicated networking session for members and friends of the ANZEP Network — a chance to meet others working in the field, build new connections, and contribute to discussions about the future of the network.

If you're attending SASP and interested in environmental psychology, climate and sustainability research, or research impact, we'd love to have you join us.

2025

Research Impact for Environmental Psychology

Wednesday 19 November 2025 · 1:00PM – 5:00PM
📍 University of Melbourne
Redmond Barry Building (115), Latham Theatre

The inaugural ANZEP pre-conference, held alongside SASP and ACPID at the University of Melbourne, brought together environmental psychology researchers from across Australia and New Zealand. The event aimed to build on the growing momentum of the field by establishing a formal network, sharing insights, identifying opportunities and challenges, and co-developing a shared research agenda focused on real-world impact and research translation.

Keynote Speakers

Dr Wade Kelly

Pathways to Impact: Building Skills for the Future of Environmental Psychology

An interactive session exploring the skills and strategies researchers need to generate impact — engaging with communities, policy, and practice, and how the ANZEP network can support members to grow their capacity.

Senior Lecturer, Researcher Development Academy, Deakin University. Specialises in developing researchers' impact and engagement capability across the career span.

Dr John Cook

Generative Debunking and the Holy Grail of Fact-checking

An exploration of how machine learning research on fallacy and contrarian claim detection can be combined with generative AI to automatically produce psychologically-informed debunkings of climate misinformation.

Senior Research Fellow, Melbourne Centre for Behaviour Change, University of Melbourne. Researches critical thinking, psychology, gamification, and AI to counter misinformation.
Lightning Talks
Session One — 2:15 pm
Who Co-produces Research with Non-academics? Exploring Profiles of Academic Scientists in Aotearoa New Zealand
Madeline Judge, University of Otago
Co-producing research with non-academics can enhance the real-world relevance and impact of scientific research. This study surveyed academic scientists in Aotearoa New Zealand (N = 316) to explore subgroups with different orientations towards co-producing research with industry, Māori iwi or hāpu, or other community groups. Latent profile analysis identified four main profiles: Traditional scientists, Industry-oriented scientists, Community-oriented scientists, and Anti-commercial scientists.
Why Climate Action is Brain Health Action
Boryana Todorova, University of Vienna
Climate change is not only an environmental crisis but also a profound threat to brain health. This talk discusses emerging evidence that climate-related factors — including heatwaves, extreme weather events, and air pollution — can have a profound impact on neurological function, mental health, and socio-cognitive capacities. Climate policies can be conceptualised as health and economic policies, generating triple co-benefits and providing a compelling, less polarising rationale for climate action.
Smart Nudges in Student Living: Cutting Heater Use by 74%
Sarah McInnes, University of Queensland
This study examined whether coupling IoT technologies with behavioural nudging can foster energy-saving behaviour in student accommodation. Deploying an IoT system across 51 student rooms, results showed a 74% reduction in electricity use among the experimental group, with no decline in reported satisfaction.
Between Ego and Eco Systems: Exploring Intellectual Humility and Outcomes Related to Climate Change
Ho Huynh, Australian National University
In two preregistered studies, intellectual humility was shown to predict increased trust in science/scientists, decreased climate scepticism, and increased support for climate immigrants, even when controlling for key personal variables such as political orientation.
Session Two — 3:15 pm
AI-facilitated Conversations on Shifts of Belief About Climate Change
David Cheney, University of Queensland
This project introduces AI-facilitated conversations that capture personal "conversion narratives" — participants engage in structured dialogues with a chatbot designed to elicit reflective accounts of belief change, combining the depth of qualitative inquiry with the scalability of digital surveys.
Mapping, Understanding and Reducing Misinformation About EVs
Matthew J. Hornsey, University of Queensland
Surveys across four countries (N = 6,341) revealed that more respondents agreed with misinformation statements about electric vehicles than disagreed. Conspiracy mentality emerged as the strongest predictor of such beliefs. Two interventions showed modest effectiveness in reducing EV misinformation with positive effects remaining at a 10-day follow-up.
The Promise and Limitations of Using GenAI to Reduce Climate Scepticism
Sam Pearson, University of Queensland
Two experiments examined whether dialogues with Generative AI can effectively reduce climate scepticism. Effects were modest, variable across studies, and susceptible to decay — underscoring the need for cautious, evidence-based integration of LLMs into climate communication strategies.
